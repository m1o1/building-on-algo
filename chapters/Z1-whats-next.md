# What's Next {-}

Look at what you have accomplished. You started with no smart contract knowledge and built a token vesting system with safe integer math and box storage, extended it with NFTs for transferable financial rights, constructed a constant product AMM with LP token mechanics, moved pool creation on-chain with a factory registry, extended the AMM with a yield farm built on the reward-per-token accumulator pattern, ran a lottery that pays out --- or gives its entrants their money back --- on randomness nobody could predict, designed a hybrid stateful/stateless limit order book with keeper bots, pushed the AVM to its limits with zero-knowledge proofs for private voting, and finished by making a contract operable: events a dashboard can subscribe to, error codes a stranger can act on, a pause an operator can throw, and a lifecycle stance chosen on day zero. Along the way, you internalized the security patterns that prevent real exploits, the MBR lifecycle that keeps contracts solvent, and the atomic group composition that makes DeFi composable. These are not toy examples --- they are the building blocks of production protocols.

Here is where to go next.

**Concentrated liquidity AMMs.** The constant product AMM in Chapter 14 is the Uniswap V2 model. The broader DeFi industry has moved toward concentrated liquidity (V3), where LPs choose price ranges for dramatically higher capital efficiency. As of this writing, no Algorand DEX has implemented a full V3-style concentrated liquidity AMM --- this is an open opportunity. Porting V3 concepts to the AVM would require creative use of box storage for tick data and careful opcode budget management for tick-crossing math.

**Lending and borrowing protocols.** Folks Finance and the now-sunset Algofi demonstrated that full lending/borrowing is possible on Algorand. Key concepts to study: overcollateralization, health factors, liquidation mechanics (calling AMM swaps via inner transactions to convert seized collateral), and interest rate models (utilization curves). These protocols compose heavily with AMMs for price oracles and liquidation execution.

**Cross-chain bridges and State Proofs.** The post-quantum section below describes State Proofs, Algorand's Falcon-signed history certificates. The practical application: building a light client on Ethereum that verifies Algorand State Proofs, enabling trustless asset transfers between chains. This is active infrastructure work in the Algorand ecosystem.

**Ecosystem integration.** This book built everything from scratch. Production applications integrate with existing protocols. Study the ABIs of Tinyman, Pact, and Folks Finance to understand how to call their contracts from yours. The ARC-56 specs for deployed contracts are your entry point --- load them with AlgoKit Utils and call methods directly.

**Off-chain infrastructure.** Production DeFi needs indexer services, event-driven backends, keeper bots, and monitoring. The Algorand Indexer REST API, Conduit data pipeline, and Nodely public endpoints provide the building blocks. Start with a Python service that watches for swap events (by parsing ARC-28 logs) and updates a price feed.

**MainNet operations.** Chapter 24 gave a contract the three things an operator needs --- a voice, an exit, and a stance; MainNet is where they get used in anger. Deployment there requires: key management (hardware wallets or HSMs, never KMD), contract verification (proving source matches deployed bytecode), monitoring and alerting, and an emergency response plan for what to do when you find a bug in an immutable contract (answer: communicate immediately, recommend users withdraw, deploy V2).

**Consensus participation and staking rewards.** Since the end of governance period 14 (Q1 2025), Algorand rewards come from consensus participation rather than quarterly governance commitments. Validators earn 10 Algo per proposed block (decaying over time) plus 50% of transaction fees. Anyone can participate in consensus by running a node and registering participation keys; the 30,000 to 70 million Algo balance window and the one-time 2-Algo keyreg fee are conditions for staking-*reward* eligibility, not for participation itself. This is Algorand's long-term economic model --- understanding it matters if you are building protocols that interact with staking (like Folks Finance's liquid staking) or if you plan to operate your own infrastructure.

**Contract migration.** Immutable contracts cannot be patched, but they can be superseded. When Tinyman migrated from V1 to V2 after the exploit, the process was: deploy V2, publicly announce a migration deadline, build a migration UI that withdraws liquidity from V1 and deposits into V2 in a single atomic group, and eventually shut down the V1 frontend while leaving the V1 contracts on-chain for anyone who still needs to withdraw. The key principle: the old contract remains functional for withdrawals indefinitely (it is immutable, after all), but new deposits are directed exclusively to V2. Plan your state schema so that migration-critical data (user balances, LP positions) can be read by the new contract via `app_global_get_ex` or reconstructed from on-chain history via the indexer.

## The Post-Quantum Road: Falcon and State Proofs {-}

One direction deserves more than a paragraph, because Algorand is already walking it. Every ordinary Algorand transaction today is signed with Ed25519, an elliptic curve scheme; so are the BN254 pairings behind Chapter 23's proofs. Shor's algorithm, running on a sufficiently powerful quantum computer, breaks both. Estimates for such a machine run from 10 to 30+ years out, but blockchains are designed to operate for decades, and the "harvest now, decrypt later" threat is already real: an adversary can record today's signed transactions and break them once the machine exists. For a system that must stay trustworthy for the lifetime of the data it secures, post-quantum preparation is engineering prudence, not speculation.

**Falcon.** Algorand's chosen instrument is Falcon (Fast Fourier Lattice-based Compact Signatures over NTRU), one of the signature schemes NIST selected for post-quantum standardization in 2022; its formal standard (FN-DSA, FIPS 206) was still forthcoming as of this writing. Its security rests on the Short Integer Solution problem over NTRU lattices, for which no efficient quantum algorithm is known --- the best quantum attacks on lattices offer only modest speedups, nothing like the exponential break Shor gives over elliptic curves. Three properties make it fit Algorand: compact signatures (~666 bytes for Falcon-512, ~1,280 for Falcon-1024, against Dilithium's 2.4--4.6KB), verification fast enough for consensus (a few FFT operations over small polynomials), and a deterministic signing mode --- developed by David Lazar and Chris Peikert --- that eliminates a class of side-channel attacks tied to randomness quality. The connection runs deep: Peikert (CSO, Algorand Foundation) and Craig Gentry (former Algorand Foundation research fellow) co-authored the 2008 GPV framework that Falcon is built on.

**State Proofs: the history is already quantum-secure.** Every 256 rounds (~12 minutes), participation nodes holding a supermajority of online stake sign a compact certificate attesting to that interval's transactions --- and they sign it with Falcon-1024, not Ed25519. A node registers its Falcon key's merkle root (the `sprfkey` field) alongside its participation keys; the individual signatures are aggregated into a compact certificate under SumHash512, a hash chosen for being cheap to prove inside a SNARK circuit; and the resulting [State Proof](https://dev.algorand.co/concepts/protocol/state-proofs/) lands on-chain, verifiable by a light client on another chain with no trusted intermediary. Even if Ed25519 falls, the chain of Falcon-signed State Proofs still authenticates every past transaction: an attacker could forge new signatures, but cannot rewrite the attested history.

**Quantum-safe transactions exist today.** The `falcon_verify` opcode (AVM v12, September 2025) verifies a Falcon-1024 signature for 1,700 opcode units --- cheaper than Ed25519's 1,900; Example 22-10 priced it and put the 1,793-byte key in a box. Consensus v42 then made Falcon-1024 a first-class account signature: a transaction may carry a PQ authorization envelope (scheme, salt, 1,793-byte public key, signature up to 1,423 bytes) at three minimum fees rather than one. The LogicSig contract-account shape that verified a Falcon signature over the transaction is the predecessor; the first Falcon-signed MainNet transaction ran on November 3, 2025 ([the technical brief](https://algorand.co/blog/technical-brief-quantum-resistant-transactions) walks through it, and [github.com/algorandfoundation/falcon-signatures](https://github.com/algorandfoundation/falcon-signatures) is the CLI). The remaining step is consensus itself: replacing the Ed25519-based VRF with a post-quantum VRF is active research, with ZKBoo/ZKB++ constructions and lattice-based proposals as the current candidates. History: done. Transactions: done. Consensus: research. The running scorecard is at [algorand.co/technology/post-quantum](https://algorand.co/technology/post-quantum).

**What it means for what you built.** Chapter 23's voting system splits cleanly under a quantum adversary. The MiMC commitments, and the reveals that open them, are hash-based, and hashes survive: Grover's algorithm buys only a quadratic speedup, manageable with larger digests. The BN254 pairing checks do not survive: Shor's algorithm breaks the curve, and with it PLONK and Groth16 verification. The quantum-resistant replacement is the STARK family --- hash-based, transparent, and post-quantum --- at the price of proofs measured in tens to hundreds of kilobytes, which today exceeds what the AVM can verify economically. That trade, proof size against quantum resistance, is an open engineering frontier on every chain, and the opcode-budget arithmetic you now know how to do is exactly the tool for evaluating it.

The same recording threat hangs over Chapter 20 and Chapter 21's delegations: a delegated LogicSig is an Ed25519 signature over a program, and once the curve falls, the key that signed it can be recovered from the public record and used to mint delegations the account holder never saw. The expiry those chapters refused to ship without is also the quantum bound --- it is the one limit on a delegation that does not depend on the signature scheme surviving.
